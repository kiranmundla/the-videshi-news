# Shrey Parikh Just Won the Scripps Spelling Bee. He's the Latest Chapter in a Story Only Indian America Could Write.

**Subheadline:** A 14-year-old from Rancho Cucamonga spelled 32 words in 90 seconds to claim the crown. Behind him stands a quarter-century of dominance that says more about the Indian diaspora than any census ever could.

---

On Thursday evening at DAR Constitution Hall in Washington, D.C., Shrey Parikh stood alone on a stage he'd been chasing for years. The 14-year-old eighth-grader from Rancho Cucamonga, California, had ninety seconds, a microphone, and a list of words most adults couldn't pronounce, let alone spell. He nailed 32 of 35 — a new spell-off record — and walked away the champion of the 98th Scripps National Spelling Bee.

His competition-ending word: *bromocriptine*, a polypeptide alkaloid derived from ergot that mimics the activity of dopamine. The kind of word that makes you reach for your phone and then put it down because you're not even sure what to Google.

The runner-up, Ishaan Gupta, a 12-year-old seventh-grader from Jersey City, New Jersey, spelled 25 words from the same list — a performance that would have been record-breaking in any other year. Both finalists are Indian American.

Of course they are.

Shrey won $52,500 in cash and prizes, Encyclopædia Britannica and Merriam-Webster materials, Delta Air Lines flight credits, and the Scripps Cup — the heavyweight belt of childhood academic achievement. He practiced five hours a day in the lead-up. He also qualified for the California state Mathcounts this year, because apparently one impossible competition at a time wasn't enough.

## The Numbers That Tell a Generational Story

Since Nupur Lala electrified the country by winning the 1999 Bee — a moment immortalized in the documentary *Spellbound* — Indian Americans have won 28 of the last 34 championships. Between 2008 and 2018, every single champion was of Indian descent. All eleven of them. In 2019, when the Bee ended in an unprecedented eight-way tie, seven of the eight co-champions were Indian American.

Let that land. South Asians make up roughly 1.5 percent of the American population. They have produced more than 80 percent of the Bee's champions over the past quarter century.

This isn't a fluke. This isn't a trend. This is a dynasty.

## It Started With Balu

The story begins in 1985, when a quiet kid named **Balu Natarajan** from Chicago won the 58th Scripps Bee by spelling *milieu*. He was the first Indian American champion. His father was a doctor who had immigrated from India. At the time, it was a curiosity — a feel-good story about an immigrant kid done good.

Nobody knew he was the first drop in a wave.

After Balu came **Rageshree Ramachandran** in 1988, spelling *elegiacal* at age 14 in Sacramento. Then a gap of over a decade. Then **Nupur Lala** in 1999, and from there, the floodgates opened: **Pratyush Buddiga**, **Sai Gunturi**, **Anurag Kashyap**, **Sameer Mishra**, **Kavya Shivashankar**, **Anamika Veeramani**, **Sukanya Roy**, **Snigdha Nandipati**, **Arvind Mahankali**. Year after year, Indian American kids stood on that stage, trophy in hand, confetti in their hair.

There were co-champions — the **Shivashankar sisters** (Kavya in 2009, Vanya in 2015) and the **Hathwar brothers** (Sriram in 2014, Jairam in 2016) — families where the Bee wasn't just a competition but a legacy.

The names changed. The pattern didn't.

## Why Indian Americans Keep Winning — And It's Not What You Think

The easy explanation is "Indian parents push their kids." It's reductive, and it misses the point entirely.

What actually happened is that the Indian American community — largely first- and second-generation immigrants who arrived on H-1B visas and built professional lives in medicine, engineering, and technology — built an **entire parallel infrastructure** for competitive spelling.

### The North South Foundation Pipeline

The **North South Foundation (NSF)**, a nonprofit started by Indian American families in 1989, runs hundreds of local spelling, vocabulary, math, science, and geography competitions across the country. These aren't casual Saturday morning events. They're rigorous, well-organized feeders into the national circuit. Kids who compete in NSF bees arrive at Scripps having already spelled against the best in their community, hundreds of times over.

The stats are striking: **11 percent of all Scripps competitors come through the NSF circuit**, despite the organization serving a tiny fraction of the American population. It's a minor-league system that produces major-league champions.

### The South Asian Spelling Bee

Then there's the **South Asian Spelling Bee (SASB)**, launched in 2008, an annual competition specifically for children of South Asian descent. Regional rounds, a national final at Rutgers University, cash prizes, and — most importantly — a community of families who treat spelling with the same intensity American families bring to travel baseball or competitive dance.

### The Invisible Network

Behind both organizations is something less visible but equally powerful: the **WhatsApp groups** of parents trading word lists and root-language breakdowns. The coaches, many of them former spellers. The study sessions at kitchen tables across Edison, Frisco, Cupertino, and Naperville. The five-hour daily practice regimen Shrey Parikh described in his post-victory interview.

Research from Psychology Today points to a deeper cultural factor: Indian families tend to hold an **incremental view of intelligence** — the belief that ability is built through effort, not fixed at birth. That mindset, combined with resources, community, and relentless preparation, produces results.

This is what sociologists call *ethnic capital* — the networks, norms, and knowledge that a community builds and passes down. Indian American families didn't just value education in the abstract. They built systems to channel that value into championships.

## The Diaspora Connection

Here's what the mainstream coverage always misses: **this is a diaspora story.**

The families who dominate the spelling bee circuit are disproportionately from **Andhra Pradesh and Telangana** — the same South Indian states that produced a generation of software engineers who came to the US in the 1990s and 2000s. The Hyderabad-to-Silicon Valley pipeline didn't just produce tech wealth. It produced a community obsessed with competitive excellence, one that saw the spelling bee as a uniquely American arena where their kids could prove themselves.

Indian American households have a median income of **$147,000** — more than double the national median. **Seventy-four percent** have college degrees. These are families that understand systems, that know how to optimize, that see a competition with clear rules and transparent outcomes and think: *we can win this.*

And they did. Over and over again.

For Indians living abroad, the spelling bee has become something more than a competition. It's a proxy for everything the diaspora values: discipline, education, community, and the belief that the next generation should go further than the last.

Every year, when an Indian American kid wins the Bee, it ripples through NRI WhatsApp groups and family dinners and video calls to grandparents in Hyderabad and Chennai. It's proof of concept — proof that the sacrifices of immigration, the loneliness of starting over in a new country, the years of building a life from scratch, produced something extraordinary.

## The Comeback Kid

Shrey Parikh's story has its own arc within this larger one. In 2024, he finished **third** — close enough to taste it, far enough to hurt. He lost a school bee that year due to illness, a setback that could have derailed a less determined kid.

Instead, he came back. He qualified again. He practiced five hours a day. And on Thursday, standing in a spell-off against another Indian American kid who was spelling at a level most adults can't comprehend, he set a record.

"They really motivated me and pushed me to become better," Shrey said afterward, thanking his family, his coaches, and his "amazing community."

That community is 4.4 million Indian Americans strong.

## So Your Kid Wants to Win the Bee: A Practical Guide

The Scripps Bee isn't just for prodigies. It's a system — and systems can be navigated. Here's how Indian American families have been doing it for decades, and how you can start.

### When to Start

**Age 6-8 (Grades 1-3):** Build the foundation. Read voraciously. Play word games. Start with the **Words of the Champions** booklet (free from Scripps, updated annually). The goal isn't competition — it's falling in love with language.

**Age 8-10 (Grades 3-5):** Begin competing. Enter your school spelling bee — this is the only path to Scripps. Schools must enroll through **spellingbee.com**. Simultaneously, join the **North South Foundation** circuit for additional competition reps.

**Age 10-13 (Grades 5-8):** Peak competition years. Most champions are 13-14. By now, daily practice should be structured: word roots (Latin, Greek, French, German), language patterns, and timed rounds.

### The Competition Path to Scripps

1. **School Bee** (Fall/Winter) → Your school must be enrolled in the Scripps program. Win your school bee.
2. **Regional Bee** (Winter/Spring) → Organized by local sponsors (newspapers, education organizations). Win your region.
3. **Scripps National Spelling Bee** (Late May, Washington D.C.) → 245-250 spellers from 50 states and several countries. Preliminary rounds, semifinals, finals.

### The Practice Circuit (Where the Real Training Happens)

- **North South Foundation (NSF)** — northsouth.org — Regional contests across the US for spelling, vocabulary, math, science, geography. The single most important feeder into competitive spelling for Indian American kids. Free or low-cost.
- **South Asian Spelling Bee (SASB)** — Regional rounds → National Final. Open to children of South Asian descent under 14.
- **Scripps Word Club App** — Official study app from Scripps. 4,000+ vocabulary words, games, and practice tools.
- **Words of the Champions** — The official Scripps study booklet. ~450 words. Updated annually (new edition available after Labor Day each year). Available free or on Amazon.
- **SpellPundit** — spellpundit.com — Founded by parents of former Bee competitors. Comprehensive word lists, practice tests, and root-word training used by many top finishers.
- **Hexco Academic** — hexco.com — Study guides, practice software, and coaching materials used by serious competitors for decades.

### What Champions Actually Do

- **5 hours/day** of practice in the months leading up to the Bee (Shrey Parikh, 2026)
- Study **word roots** — Latin, Greek, French, German, and other language origins. Understanding roots lets you spell words you've never seen before.
- Practice **under pressure** — timed rounds, mock bees, asking for language of origin and part of speech (just like the real Bee)
- Build a **word notebook** — every missed word, every new root, every pattern
- **Compete constantly** — NSF, SASB, school bees, local library bees, online bees. Reps matter more than raw talent.
