## Why Northeast India?

If you have visited India a dozen times and always ended up in the same Delhi-Jaipur-Goa-Kerala loop, the Northeast is the trip that will make India feel like a foreign country again. This is the India that most NRIs — and most Indians, frankly — have never seen. Seven sisters, eight states, and a cultural landscape so distinct from mainland India that crossing into Meghalaya or Nagaland feels like stepping into Southeast Asia. The food is different (pork and bamboo shoot, not paneer and dal). The faces are different. The churches outnumber the temples. It is the most thrilling, confounding, and rewarding part of India to explore, and it is finally becoming accessible.

For the diaspora specifically, Northeast India forces you to confront how narrow your idea of "India" might be. This is where the world's wettest place sits under a permanent curtain of rain, where living root bridges have been grown — not built — over centuries, where one-horned rhinos wander through elephant grass taller than your car, and where a state called Sikkim gives you Himalayan peaks that rival Nepal without the altitude sickness of Ladakh. If you are the kind of NRI who has "done" India and thinks there is nothing left to surprise you, the Northeast exists specifically to prove you wrong.

## Top Experiences

**1. Living Root Bridges, Meghalaya** — The double-decker root bridge at Nongriat village near Cherrapunji requires a 3,500-step descent (and ascent), but it is one of India's most extraordinary sights — a bridge grown from the aerial roots of rubber fig trees over 150+ years. Start early, carry 2 litres of water, and wear proper shoes. You can swim in the natural pool below the bridge. The nearby Nohkalikai Falls, India's tallest plunge waterfall at 1,115 feet, is a 20-minute drive from Cherrapunji.

**2. Kaziranga National Park, Assam** — Home to two-thirds of the world's one-horned rhinoceros population, Kaziranga is India's answer to an African safari but without the price tag. Elephant safaris start at dawn (₹1,500 for Indian nationals, ₹3,000 for foreigners) and jeep safaris run through three ranges — the Central Range (Kohora) is the most popular for rhino sightings. The park is open November through April; book safaris through your resort or at the park office. You will also see wild elephants, water buffalo, and if you are extraordinarily lucky, a Bengal tiger.

**3. Gangtok & Tsomgo Lake, Sikkim** — Gangtok is a beautifully maintained hill town with sweeping views of Kanchenjunga, the world's third-highest peak. The Rumtek Monastery, 24 km outside town, is Sikkim's largest and most important Buddhist monastery. Tsomgo Lake at 12,400 feet is a frozen alpine lake surrounded by prayer flags — absolutely stunning but check weather conditions before going, as the road closes frequently. MG Marg, the pedestrian boulevard in central Gangtok, is one of the cleanest and most pleasant market streets in all of India.

**4. Tawang Monastery, Arunachal Pradesh** — The second-largest Buddhist monastery in the world after Lhasa's Potala Palace sits at 10,000 feet in the remote western corner of Arunachal Pradesh. Getting there is an ordeal — a 12-hour drive from Tezpur through the Sela Pass at 13,700 feet — but the monastery, the war memorial, and the sheer isolation of Tawang make it unforgettable. This is the trip you brag about at dinner parties. Requires an Inner Line Permit (see NRI Tips below).

**5. Mawlynnong & Dawki, Meghalaya** — Mawlynnong was declared Asia's cleanest village, and it genuinely lives up to the title. Glass-bottomed boats on the Umngot River at Dawki reveal water so clear the boats appear to float on air — you have seen the photos on Instagram, and yes, it really looks like that. Best visited November through March for the clearest water. The two spots are about an hour apart and can be done in one day from Shillong.

**6. Majuli River Island, Assam** — The world's largest river island sits in the Brahmaputra and is a centre of Vaishnavite culture. Ancient satras (monasteries) practice a living tradition of dance, music, and mask-making. Take the ferry from Jorhat (1.5 hours) and spend a night. The island is slowly shrinking due to erosion, lending the visit a bittersweet urgency.

## Getting There

There are no nonstop flights from the US to the Northeast. Fly into Delhi or Kolkata, then catch domestic connections. Guwahati (GAU) is the main gateway — IndiGo and Air India operate multiple daily flights from Delhi (2.5 hours) and Kolkata (1.5 hours). From Guwahati, Kaziranga is a 4.5-hour drive. For Meghalaya, Shillong is 3 hours by road from Guwahati (the Shillong airport has limited service). For Sikkim, fly to Bagdogra (IXB) near Siliguri and drive 4.5 hours to Gangtok — or take the new Pakyong Airport near Gangtok if flights are operating (service is weather-dependent and unreliable). For Arunachal, drive from Guwahati or Tezpur. Internal roads are dramatically better than they were five years ago, but still expect slow going on mountain routes. Hiring a local SUV with driver is the smartest move — rates run ₹3,000–₹5,000/day depending on the vehicle and route.

## Where to Stay

The Northeast does not have the luxury hotel density of Rajasthan or Kerala, but options have improved. In Guwahati, the Radisson Blu ($80–$100/night) and Novotel Guwahati GS Road ($85–$100/night) are the best full-service options. In Shillong, the Courtyard by Marriott ($100–$125/night) is surprisingly excellent, and the Ri Kynjai resort on Umiam Lake ($120–$180/night) is the most atmospheric stay in Meghalaya. At Kaziranga, Resort Borgos ($50–$70/night) and Infinity Kaziranga ($60–$90/night) put you right at the park gates. Diphlu River Lodge ($150–$250/night) is the luxury pick. In Gangtok, the Elgin Nor-Khill ($80–$120/night) is a heritage property with character, and the Summit Namnang Courtyard & Spa ($50–$80/night) is solid mid-range. Budget guesthouses across the region run $15–$35/night and are generally clean if basic. For Cherrapunji, the Polo Cherrapunjee Resort ($100–$150/night) offers the most comfortable stay near the root bridges. Book everything well in advance for the October–November peak season.

## NRI Tips

This is where documentation gets complicated, so pay attention. **Assam, Meghalaya, Sikkim, and Tripura** do not require any special permits for Indian passport or OCI holders. **Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram, and Manipur** require an Inner Line Permit (ILP) for Indian citizens and a Protected Area Permit (PAP) for OCI/foreign passport holders. The ILP can be obtained online at arunachalilp.com for Indian passport holders (processed in a few hours, valid 15 days). OCI cardholders with foreign passports must apply for a PAP, which requires booking through a registered travel agency with a minimum group of two — plan this at least 4–6 weeks in advance through the Ministry of Home Affairs or the state's Resident Commissioner office in Delhi. The PAP costs around $30. Do not show up at the Arunachal border without your permit — you will be turned back.

Get a Jio or Airtel SIM in Guwahati; network coverage is patchy to nonexistent in remote Meghalaya and Arunachal. BSNL still has the best coverage in the deep Northeast, but getting a BSNL SIM as an NRI is bureaucratic. Carry cash — UPI works in Guwahati and Gangtok but is unreliable elsewhere. The Northeast monsoon (June–September) brings biblical rainfall — Cherrapunji averages 11,000 mm annually — so the ideal window is October through April. Pack layers: Gangtok and Tawang get genuinely cold in winter, while Guwahati and Kaziranga can be warm and humid. Malaria prophylaxis is recommended for low-altitude areas in Assam and Arunachal. Food in the Northeast is heavily non-vegetarian — pure vegetarians will need to plan meals carefully outside of major towns. The people of the Northeast are exceptionally welcoming, but be culturally sensitive — do not call the region "exotic" or compare it to China/Southeast Asia, and always ask before photographing tribal communities.
