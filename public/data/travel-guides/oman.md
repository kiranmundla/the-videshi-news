## Why Oman?

Oman is the Gulf state that India forgot to talk about. While Dubai gets all the attention and Abu Dhabi gets the cricket, Oman has been quietly maintaining centuries-old ties with India — ties that run deeper than any other Gulf nation. The Indian community in Oman numbers over 700,000, making it the largest expatriate group in the country. Omani cuisine borrows heavily from Indian flavors. Hindi and Malayalam are widely spoken in Muscat's markets. The historic dhow trade routes between Muscat and Kerala, Gujarat, and Sindh go back over a thousand years. For NRIs, visiting Oman feels less like a foreign trip and more like visiting a cousin you did not know you had.

Beyond the cultural connection, Oman offers something the rest of the Gulf cannot: raw, unspoiled natural beauty. Dramatic fjords in Musandam, the vast Empty Quarter desert, ancient mountain villages in Jebel Akhdar, and pristine beaches with sea turtles nesting under moonlight. Oman has deliberately avoided the hyper-development of Dubai, choosing instead to preserve its heritage and landscape. The result is a destination that feels authentic, safe, and profoundly peaceful — a Gulf experience for people who find Dubai exhausting.

## Top Experiences

**1. Sultan Qaboos Grand Mosque, Muscat** — One of the most beautiful mosques in the world, and one of the few in the Gulf open to non-Muslim visitors (Saturday–Thursday, 8 AM–11 AM). The main prayer hall holds the world's second-largest hand-woven carpet (4.2 million knots) and a Swarovski crystal chandelier weighing 8 tons. Free entry. Dress modestly — women must cover hair, arms, and legs; abayas are available at the entrance.

**2. Wadi Shab** — A stunning canyon hike that ends at an emerald pool fed by a waterfall hidden inside a cave. You must swim through the final pool and squeeze through a rock gap to reach the secret waterfall — it is an adventure. Hire a boat to cross the initial wadi (OMR 1, about $2.60). Bring water shoes, a dry bag for your phone, and start early to avoid the afternoon heat. The hike is 4 km one way, moderate difficulty.

**3. Musandam Peninsula (Dhow Cruise)** — Often called the "Norway of Arabia," Musandam's limestone fjords (khors) plunge into deep blue water. Take a full-day dhow cruise from Khasab ($35–50/person including lunch), stopping for swimming and snorkeling. Dolphins frequently ride the bow wave. Note: Musandam is an Omani exclave separated from the rest of Oman by the UAE — you will drive through UAE territory (passport required).

**4. Nizwa Fort & Souq** — The cultural heart of interior Oman, Nizwa was once the capital. The 17th-century fort has a massive round tower with views across the date palm oasis. Visit the adjacent souq on Friday morning for the famous livestock auction — goats, cattle, and camels are traded in a chaotic, fascinating scene. Entry to the fort is OMR 5 ($13).

**5. Jebel Akhdar (Green Mountain)** — A 2,000-meter-high plateau in the Al Hajar mountains, known for rose gardens, terraced orchards, and a cool climate that defies Gulf expectations. The winding mountain road requires a 4WD vehicle (police checkpoint enforces this). The Diana's Point viewpoint offers a 1,000-meter drop into a canyon. The Anantara Al Jabal Al Akhdar resort perches on the cliff edge — even a lunch visit is worth the drive.

**6. Ras Al Jinz Turtle Reserve** — Oman is one of the few places on earth where you can watch endangered green sea turtles nest on the beach. Night tours (OMR 7/$18) run year-round, with peak nesting season from June to September. The reserve is basic but the experience of watching a two-meter turtle drag herself onto the sand to lay eggs is profound. Book through the official reserve — no freelance guides.

## Getting There

No direct flights operate between the US and Muscat. The best connections route through Abu Dhabi (Etihad), Doha (Qatar Airways), or Istanbul (Turkish Airlines). From New York JFK, round-trip fares run $1,100–1,600 via Etihad through Abu Dhabi, with a total travel time of 16–18 hours. From the West Coast, Turkish Airlines via Istanbul is competitive at $1,000–1,400 from LAX or SFO. Oman Air operates excellent service from connecting Gulf hubs. Within Oman, driving is the best way to explore — roads are outstanding, well-signed, and empty outside Muscat. Rent a car at Muscat airport from Europcar or Budget ($30–50/day); get a 4WD if you plan to visit Jebel Akhdar or Wahiba Sands.

## Where to Stay

**Luxury:** The Chedi Muscat ($300–600/night) is Oman's most iconic luxury hotel — 21 acres of gardens, three pools, and an elegance that Dubai's flashy towers cannot match. Anantara Al Jabal Al Akhdar Resort ($350–700) offers cliff-edge luxury at 2,000 meters. The Alila Jabal Akhdar ($280–500) is a stunning alternative on the same mountain plateau.

**Mid-Range:** Mysk Al Mouj by Shaza ($120–200) in Muscat's modern Al Mouj district is excellent. In Nizwa, the Golden Tulip ($80–130) puts you near the fort and souq. The Sur Plaza Hotel ($70–110) is basic but clean and well-located for Ras Al Jinz turtle reserve visits.

**Budget:** Muscat has limited budget options compared to other Gulf cities. Centro Capital Centre by Rotana ($60–90) offers reliable value. In Nizwa, Antique Inn ($40–60) is a converted traditional house. Desert camps in Wahiba Sands range from OMR 15 ($40) for basic Bedouin tents to OMR 50 ($130) for glamping with en-suite bathrooms.

## NRI Tips

US passport holders receive a free visa on arrival for stays up to 14 days (available at the airport e-gate or immigration counter). Indian passport holders need a visa — apply online through the Royal Oman Police portal ($20 for a 10-day tourist visa, processed in 1–3 days). OCI card holders still need a visa based on their passport country. The currency is the Omani Rial (OMR), pegged to the USD at roughly 1 OMR = $2.60 — making Oman's prices deceptively high (a OMR 5 coffee is actually $13). Credit cards are accepted at hotels and larger shops, but carry cash for souqs and small towns. Oman is extremely safe — it consistently ranks among the safest countries in the world. Dress modestly outside resort areas, especially at mosques and in rural villages. Alcohol is available only at licensed hotels. Summer (June–September) is brutally hot, with temperatures exceeding 45°C in the interior. Visit between October and March for ideal weather (25–35°C). Oman is a Muslim country — during Ramadan, eating in public during daylight hours is illegal. The Indian community means you will find excellent Indian restaurants in Muscat; try Mumtaz Mahal or Calcutta restaurant for familiar flavors. Oman uses the same plug type (Type G) as India and the UK.
