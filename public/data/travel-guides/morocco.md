## Why Morocco?

Morocco is the sensory overload trip — the one where every alley in the medina reveals a new color, smell, sound, and texture. Saffron-stained tagines bubbling in copper pots, the azaan echoing across the tiled rooftops of Fez, artisans hammering brass lanterns in souks that have not changed in 600 years, and the vast silence of the Sahara dunes glowing orange at sunset. For NRIs, Morocco occupies a fascinating cultural middle ground: it is an Islamic country with a warmth and hospitality that feels distinctly familiar to Indians, yet its aesthetic — Moorish geometric tiles, French colonial architecture, Berber textiles — is utterly different from anything in South Asia. It is exoticism without the alienation.

Morocco is also one of the easiest African countries to visit from the US. Direct flights from New York to Casablanca take just 7 hours — shorter than flying to London. The tourism infrastructure is excellent, with everything from $30/night riads to palatial five-star properties. The country is safe, the food is spectacular (and largely vegetarian-friendly — Moroccan cuisine relies heavily on vegetables, couscous, legumes, and preserved lemons), and the variety within a small country is remarkable. You can go from the medieval medina of Fez to the blue streets of Chefchaouen to the Sahara Desert to the Atlas Mountains in a single week. Few countries pack this much visual drama into such a compact itinerary.

## Top Experiences

**1. Jemaa el-Fnaa, Marrakech** — The most famous public square in Africa comes alive at dusk: snake charmers, henna artists, acrobats, storytellers, and rows of food stalls serving everything from snail soup to fresh-squeezed orange juice (10 MAD/$1). Watch the chaos from a rooftop terrace at Café des Épices or Le Grand Balcon du Café Glacier. During the day, explore the surrounding souks — the dyers' quarter, the spice alley, the leather tanneries. Bargain hard and enjoy the theatre of it.

**2. Fez Medina (Fes el-Bali)** — The world's largest car-free urban area, a 1,200-year-old labyrinth of 9,000 streets where donkeys are still the primary mode of transport. The Chouara Tannery is the visual centerpiece — watch leather being dyed in stone vats using methods unchanged since medieval times (the smell is intense; accept the sprig of mint offered at the entrance). The Al Quaraouiyine Mosque houses the world's oldest continuously operating university (founded 859 AD). Hire a guide — you will get lost without one. Official guides charge 400–600 MAD ($40–60) for a half-day.

**3. Sahara Desert — Merzouga & Erg Chebbi** — A night in the Sahara is a rite of passage. Ride a camel into the Erg Chebbi dunes at sunset, dine under the stars at a desert camp, and wake to a sunrise that paints the sand in shades of pink, gold, and copper. Luxury desert camps (Merzouga Luxury Desert Camp, Erg Chebbi Luxury Camp) run $100–250/person including meals, camel ride, and entertainment. Budget camps start at $30–50/person. The drive from Marrakech is 9 hours — most visitors take a 2-day/1-night tour via the Dades and Todra Gorges.

**4. Chefchaouen — The Blue Pearl** — A mountain town in the Rif where every building, alley, and staircase is painted in shades of blue. The origin is debated — Jewish tradition, mosquito repellent, or simply aesthetic choice — but the photographic result is staggering. Walk the medina without a map, visit the Ras el-Maa waterfall at the edge of town, and hike the Rif Mountains for panoramic views. Stay overnight — day-trippers miss the magic of evening light on blue walls. Hotels are cheap: $30–80/night for a beautiful riad.

**5. Hassan II Mosque, Casablanca** — The third-largest mosque in the world, built on a promontory over the Atlantic Ocean. The minaret is 210 meters tall — the tallest religious structure in the world. It is one of the few mosques in Morocco open to non-Muslim visitors (guided tours: 130 MAD/$13, multiple daily sessions, closed Friday mornings). The architecture, combining Italian marble, carved cedarwood, and zellige tilework, is breathtaking. Even if Casablanca is otherwise skippable, this mosque is not.

**6. Atlas Mountains — Imlil & Toubkal** — The gateway to North Africa's highest peak, Mount Toubkal (4,167m). Even if you are not a mountaineer, the Berber village of Imlil (1.5 hours from Marrakech) offers stunning trekking through walnut groves, terraced farms, and mountain valleys. A guided day hike from Imlil to Aroumd village costs 300–500 MAD ($30–50) including lunch at a Berber home. For the summit, the 2-day Toubkal trek is challenging but non-technical — hire a guide and mule through a local agency ($150–200/person all-inclusive).

## Getting There

Royal Air Maroc operates direct flights from New York JFK to Casablanca (CMN) — just 7 hours, one of the shortest transatlantic routes. Round-trip fares run $500–900. From Washington Dulles, the same route runs via a brief Casablanca stop. From the West Coast, connect through New York, Paris (Air France), or London (British Airways). Casablanca is Morocco's main international gateway, but Marrakech Menara Airport (RAK) also receives direct flights from Europe — useful if starting there. The ONCF rail network connects Casablanca to Marrakech (2.5 hours, first class 200 MAD/$20), Fez (4 hours), Tangier (2 hours via high-speed Al Boraq), and Rabat (1 hour). Trains are punctual and comfortable. For the Sahara, Atlas Mountains, and Chefchaouen, rent a car or book organized transport — CTM buses are reliable and cheap.

## Where to Stay

**Luxury:** Royal Mansour Marrakech ($2,000–4,000/night) is widely considered the finest hotel in Africa — each accommodation is a private three-story riad with a plunge pool. La Mamounia ($500–1,000) is the legendary palace hotel where Churchill painted in the gardens. Selman Marrakech ($400–800) features Arabesque architecture and Arabian horses on the grounds.

**Mid-Range:** La Maison Arabe in Marrakech ($180–300) is a heritage riad with a cooking school and pool. Riad Fes in Fez ($120–220) is a beautifully restored riad overlooking the medina. Ksar Anika in Marrakech ($130–200) offers boutique charm with breakfast included.

**Budget:** Morocco has the best budget accommodation in North Africa. Beautiful riads with courtyards, tiled rooms, and rooftop terraces start at $30–60/night in Marrakech and Fez. In Chefchaouen, Dar Echchaouen ($35–60) has blue-washed rooms with mountain views. Hostels like Equity Point in Marrakech run $10–20/bed. In the Sahara, basic desert camps start at $30/person including dinner, camel ride, and sunrise.

## NRI Tips

US passport holders do not need a visa for Morocco — you receive a 90-day entry stamp on arrival. Indian passport holders also do not need a visa for stays up to 90 days — Morocco is one of the few countries offering visa-free access to Indian passport holders. OCI card holders follow their passport country's rules. Currency is the Moroccan Dirham (MAD); 1 USD ≈ 10 MAD. Cash is king — ATMs are plentiful in cities but carry cash for souks, small towns, and rural areas. Bargaining is expected and enjoyed in souks — start at 30–40% of the asking price and work up. French is the primary second language; English is spoken in tourist areas but limited in smaller towns. Morocco is Muslim — dress modestly outside resort areas, especially in the medina and rural villages. Alcohol is available at licensed restaurants and hotels but not widely in the medina. Vegetarian food is widely available: vegetable tagines, couscous with seven vegetables, harira soup, msemen flatbread, and fresh salads are staples. Ask for "bidoun l7am" (without meat). Mint tea is offered everywhere — refusing is considered rude. The biggest scam to watch for: "helpful" strangers leading you through the medina then demanding payment, faux guides, and inflated taxi prices. Use Petit Taxi (metered city cabs, insist on the meter) and agree on prices before any service. Best time to visit: March–May and September–November. Summer (June–August) is brutally hot inland, especially in Marrakech and the Sahara (40°C+). Ramadan affects restaurant availability during daylight hours but sunset iftars are a beautiful cultural experience.
