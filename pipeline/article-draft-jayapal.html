A clash over legal immigration

Two Democratic lawmakers from Washington state on Thursday sharply criticised the Trump administration's decision to suspend Microsoft and seven other technology companies from a key employment-based green-card programme, warning that it threatens the legal immigration pathways skilled foreign professionals rely on and could damage American innovation.

The action affects the Permanent Labour Certification programme, known as PERM, which employers use to sponsor foreign workers for certain employment-based green cards. The eight suspended companies — including Microsoft, Adobe and several Indian IT firms — can no longer access it, part of a broader crackdown that has also included a $100,000 H-1B filing fee and a lawsuit by unions and universities over new USCIS green-card rules.

What the PERM suspension actually does

PERM is the mandatory first step in most employer-sponsored cases: the Department of Labor must certify that no qualified American worker is available for the position. With that step blocked, the affected employers cannot initiate new labour certification applications, leaving workers whose sponsorships had not yet begun with no path to an employment-based green card through their current employer. The move does not automatically cancel existing H-1B visas.

Jayapal's case

Jayapal — the Indian-American lawmaker representing a Seattle-area district that includes tens of thousands of Microsoft workers — spoke as the ranking Democrat on the House immigration subcommittee. She says she is the only member of Congress to have once held an H-1B visa herself.

You don't fix a broken immigration system by taking a hammer to legal immigration.

— Rep. Pramila Jayapal, ranking Democrat, House immigration subcommittee

Jayapal argued that PERM provides "a clear process with steps to ensure that this is a net benefit to our economy and does not harm American jobs." "Targeting Microsoft or the PERM program in this way simply undermines legal immigration and weakens America's ability to innovate," she said in her October 8 statement.

The administration's answer

The rebuke drew an immediate retort from the top of the administration. Vice President J.D. Vance, who has spearheaded the crackdown on foreign-worker visas, mocked Jayapal's defence of the Redmond giant, writing that "'socialism' apparently means fighting like hell to ensure megacorporations have a never-ending flow of cheap labour."

The Trump administration's action today is an attack on current H-1B visa holders already in the United States.

— Rep. Suzan DelBene, Democrat, Washington

"Haphazardly taking away the pathway to a lawful green card for skilled workers at certain American companies is a biased and punitive misuse of the law," DelBene said, arguing the restrictions would do nothing to address alleged H-1B abuse while injecting "even more uncertainty into our already turbulent economy."

What it means for Indian professionals

The fallout lands hardest on Indian professionals, who make up the largest share of employment-based green-card applicants. The October visa bulletin restored movement in the EB-2 India category, but the overall green-card wait for Indians still stretches past 11 years — and losing the PERM on-ramp at eight major employers removes one of the few reliable starting gates to permanent residency.

Microsoft, headquartered in Redmond, Washington, employs a large Indian and Indian-American workforce. For H-1B holders at the suspended firms, the practical question is where they stand in the sponsorship timeline: workers with an approved PERM and an I-140 petition already filed are in a different position from those whose employers had only planned to begin sponsorship. Immigration lawyers advise affected workers to confirm the status of any filed applications — a change of employer generally means restarting the PERM process. New Delhi has said US green-card curbs "do not advance" the two countries' shared ambitions.

What comes next

Unions and universities have already sued the administration over related USCIS rules, and the PERM action could face its own legal challenge — particularly over whether singling out specific companies for exclusion from a statutory programme survives judicial review. The next test will be whether any of the suspended companies mounts a challenge.
