# Elle Review: The Legally Blonde Prequel Nobody Asked For Turns Out to Be the Summer's Guilty Pleasure

Amazon's origin story takes us back to 1995 Seattle, where a young Elle Woods was already bending every rule — one pink outfit at a time.

---

Let us begin with a confession: when Amazon announced a *Legally Blonde* prequel series, the collective groan from the internet could be heard from Malibu to Mumbai. The 2001 film starring Reese Witherspoon is one of those rare comedies that achieved perfection by accident — a candy-pink feminist manifesto disguised as a sorority comedy, quotable from its first frame to its last. Making a prequel felt less like storytelling and more like corporate necromancy, the streaming equivalent of digging up a beloved pet and asking it to do tricks.

And yet. *Elle*, which dropped on Amazon Prime Video on July 1, is — against every reasonable expectation — rather delightful.

The series follows a teenage Elle Woods (Lexi Minetree, a genuine discovery) in 1995 Seattle, years before Harvard Law and the bend-and-snap heard 'round the world. This Elle is fifteen, relentlessly optimistic, navigating the peculiar horrors of mid-nineties American high school with the same weaponised cheerfulness that Witherspoon would later make iconic. The grunge capital of the world is an inspired setting — there's something deeply funny about a girl in head-to-toe pink navigating a city that practically invented looking miserable in flannel.

## Minetree Is the Real Deal

The series lives or dies on its lead, and Minetree carries it with startling confidence. She doesn't do a Witherspoon impression — thank God — but instead finds the character's emotional core: the stubborn refusal to be diminished, the genuine intelligence behind the blonde hair, the radical kindness that the original film turned into a superpower. Minetree's Elle is younger, rawer, occasionally insecure in ways that Witherspoon's never was, and the vulnerability makes her more interesting, not less.

The pilot episode opens with Elle's family relocating from Los Angeles to Seattle — her father's law firm has a new office — and the culture shock is played for comedy without ever tipping into cruelty. Elle's first day at a school where everyone worships Nirvana and considers smiling a sign of intellectual weakness is genuinely funny, a fish-out-of-water story that earns its laughs because the fish has such impeccable taste.

## The Nineties, Rendered With Love and Accuracy

The production design deserves its own paragraph. The show's recreation of mid-nineties Seattle — the coffee shops before they were "artisanal," the pre-internet social hierarchies, the specific shade of existential ennui that passed for teenage sophistication — is meticulous without being museum-piece precious. The soundtrack is predictably excellent (Hole, TLC, No Doubt), deployed with enough restraint that it complements scenes rather than curating a Spotify playlist.

Where *Elle* stumbles is where most prequels do: the compulsive need to plant seeds. There are moments — a law textbook spotted on a shelf, a courtroom scene watched through a window, a throwaway line about Harvard — that feel less like organic storytelling and more like the writers winking so aggressively they've given themselves a migraine. We know where Elle Woods ends up. We don't need breadcrumbs every eight minutes.

## What NRI Audiences Will Recognise

Here's what makes *Elle* unexpectedly resonant for the Indian diaspora: it's fundamentally a story about being underestimated because of how you look, where you're from, and what people assume about your intelligence based on your presentation. Every NRI kid who showed up to an American school and was immediately slotted into someone else's assumptions will recognise something in Elle's stubborn insistence on being exactly who she is. The specifics are different — pink Prada versus kurta comments — but the emotional architecture is identical.

The show has already been renewed for a second season, which suggests Amazon knows it has something. At eight episodes of roughly thirty minutes each, *Elle* is a breezy, bingeable watch that respects its source material without genuflecting before it. It won't replace the original film in anyone's heart, and it isn't trying to. What it does, with considerable charm and a star-making central performance, is remind you why Elle Woods mattered in the first place — not because she was funny, though she was, but because she refused to let anyone else's limitations become her own.

Stream it this weekend. You'll finish it in an afternoon and spend the evening texting your friends about it. That's the highest compliment a summer show can earn.

*Elle* is streaming now on Amazon Prime Video.
