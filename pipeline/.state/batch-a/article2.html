What: Pakistan rules out an immediate military response under the Mecca pact after Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabia. When: Thursday, September 10. Where: Islamabad; the Houthis struck Saudi cities and energy infrastructure this week. Why it matters: The first real test of the new trilateral defence pact is exposing how far Pakistan will go for its Gulf ally.

Pakistan said on Thursday that there were no discussions about a military reaction from Islamabad under the Mecca defence agreement in response to the Houthis' attacks on Saudi Arabia — but added that Pakistan would act when the "time comes."

"There is no such thing under discussion right now ... When time comes (we) will act under the agreement," foreign ministry spokesperson Sajjad Haider Khan said at a weekly media briefing, when asked how Islamabad planned to fulfil its obligations under the joint defence accord signed with Turkey and Saudi Arabia.

The pact and this week's attacks

The joint defence agreement, signed last month in Mecca, stipulates that an armed attack on any one of the three countries will be regarded as an attack on all of them. The question of Pakistan's obligations arose after Iran-backed Houthis struck Saudi cities and energy infrastructure this week — attacks that by Thursday had, in Reuters' description, turned the confrontation into a second theatre of war in the Middle East.

Reuters reported that Islamabad has warned Iran to rein in its Yemeni Houthi allies after they stepped up attacks on Saudi Arabia. Pakistan, which has also been mediating the conflict between the United States and Iran, condemned the attacks but provided no details on how it might respond under the terms of its defence pact. Khan also said there were no plans to expand the Mecca alliance, as Washington struggles to contain the regional upheaval that has disrupted global energy supplies.

A careful balancing act

Islamabad's measured response reflects the tightrope it walks. Pakistan has deep security and economic ties with Saudi Arabia — including a large Pakistani workforce in the kingdom — but it also shares a long border with Iran and has historically avoided being drawn into Tehran's rivalries in the Gulf. A formal military commitment against the Houthis would risk rupturing Pakistan's relationship with Iran at a moment when Islamabad is positioning itself as a mediator between Washington and Tehran.

The escalation is already being felt in markets. Brent crude jumped more than 5% to $106.60 a barrel on Thursday, while US crude climbed above $100 for the first time since May, according to Reuters — a reminder of how quickly Red Sea and Gulf tensions translate into fuel costs worldwide.

Diaspora angle

For the Indian diaspora, the stakes are direct: millions of Indians live and work in Saudi Arabia and the wider Gulf, and any widening of the conflict threatens both their safety and the remittance flows that sustain families back home. Rising crude prices also feed straight into India's import bill and domestic fuel costs.

What comes next

The key variable is whether the Houthis sustain or escalate their strikes on Saudi targets. Continued attacks would intensify pressure on Riyadh to invoke the pact formally — forcing Pakistan to decide what "when time comes" actually means. Watch for Pakistan's back-channel diplomacy with Tehran, any movement on expanding or activating the trilateral alliance, and Washington's efforts to contain a conflict that is already spilling into global energy markets.
