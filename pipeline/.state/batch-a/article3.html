What: Commerce secretary says the India-US trade deal is "more or less" finalised, with signing to follow once remaining issues are resolved. When: Wednesday, September 9. Where: Global Fintech Fest 2026, Mumbai. Why it matters: The pact would give Indian exporters preferential access to their largest market after years of stop-start negotiations.

The long-awaited India-US trade deal has been "more or less" finalised, Commerce Secretary Rajesh Agrawal said on Wednesday, adding that the two countries are now working on a framework for preferential market access before signing the agreement at an "appropriate time."

Speaking to the media on the sidelines of the Global Fintech Fest 2026 in Mumbai, Agrawal said a few issues were still being discussed between the two sides. "There are few things which both sides are discussing, but it will be signed at an appropriate time," he said, as quoted by news agency ANI.

What the commerce secretary said

Agrawal explained that the challenge is structural: India operates on most-favoured-nation tariffs while the US uses executive tariffs, requiring an architecture that creates tariff differentials and preferential market access for India.

"From their (US) side, they are operating on executive tariff. So, they are building an architecture which will create that differentials and create a preferential market access. So, I think at an opportune time, both should be able to sign," Agrawal said. He added that businesses in both countries were already engaging strongly and were satisfied with the overall understanding reached between the two governments.

"There are few things which both sides are discussing, but it will be signed at an appropriate time."

— Rajesh Agrawal, Commerce Secretary of India

The road to this point

The announcement marks the most concrete signal yet that negotiations are nearing the finish line after years of ebbs and flows. The two countries issued a joint statement on February 7 agreeing on a framework for an interim agreement on mutually beneficial trade, but had yet to finalise the first phase.

Sticking points have included opening India's sensitive agricultural sector to US genetically modified products, alongside steep US reciprocal tariffs, a Section 301 probe, and a new Russia-linked bill. Last week, Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal said India would finalise the deal once Washington offered preferential treatment compared with its peers.

Other deals and export momentum

Agrawal also outlined the status of India's other trade negotiations. The India-Chile free trade agreement is at the final stage, with some areas still under discussion; he said the next two to three months should bring progress. India is hoping to make the India-New Zealand free trade agreement live "as soon as possible," potentially in October, he said.

On freight costs affecting exporters, Agrawal said the government was working with exporters to ensure access to ships and containers and minimise cargo delays and costs. Exports grew by more than 15% in the first four months, he said, indicating that the impact of higher freight costs had not been as large as anticipated.

Services, fintech and remittances

Agrawal used the fintech stage to press a broader point: Indian industry needs to diversify its services exports beyond IT and IT-enabled services, which account for about 50% of services exports, and professional services, which contribute another 30%. India's total exports stood at $863 billion in 2025-26, with services contributing $421 billion.

The global financial services market is worth around $670 billion, while India's share is about $8 billion — just over 1%, 95% of it digitally delivered. "Fintechs here can make the change," Agrawal said. He also noted that cutting remittance costs for Indian migrant workers from 5-6% to 3% could put an additional $5 billion in their hands.

Diaspora angle

For the Indian diaspora, the deal has two tangible hooks. Indian-American businesses and exporters stand to gain preferential access to the US market — the clearest commercial upside in years — while millions of NRIs could benefit if fintech-driven remittance costs fall as Agrawal envisions, leaving more money with migrant workers' families.

What comes next

Watch for the formal announcement of the signing date and, more importantly, the details of the tariff architecture: which Indian goods get preferential US access, and at what cost. With the Chile and New Zealand agreements also nearing completion, the coming months could mark the busiest period for Indian trade diplomacy in a decade.
